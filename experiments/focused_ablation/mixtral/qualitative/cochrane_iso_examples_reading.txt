ORIGINAL: We reviewed 11 studies with 414 participants. Some studies compared different types of ultrasound therapy, while others combined ultrasound with other treatments. The quality of these studies was low in some cases.

Only two studies looked at short-term overall improvement after treatment. A low-quality study with 68 participants suggested that ultrasound might improve symptoms when compared to a placebo. However, this result should be interpreted carefully due to high dropout rates and other issues. Another low-quality study with 60 participants found that adding ultrasound to splint therapy improved patient satisfaction at three months, but it decreased the chance of improvement when compared to laser therapy plus splint.

Differences in ultrasound frequency, intensity, and combination with other treatments showed little or no significant impact on symptoms, function, or neurophysiologic parameters. Only three studies reported adverse effects, making it difficult to draw conclusions about the safety of ultrasound therapy.

There is poor evidence suggesting that ultrasound may be more effective than a placebo for short- or long-term symptom improvement in carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). There is insufficient support for one type of ultrasound regimen over another, and there is no clear proof that ultrasound therapy is more effective than other non-surgical treatments like splinting, exercises, and oral drugs. More rigorous studies are needed to determine the effectiveness and safety of ultrasound therapy for CTS.

ORIGINAL: Two studies (including 116 women) compared home and hospital management for preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM). However, each trial had too few participants to thoroughly assess most outcomes. Women were monitored for 2 to 3 days before randomization.

There was not enough evidence to say if perinatal mortality differed between the two groups (RR 1.93, 95% CI 0.19 to 20.05). There were no differences in serious neonatal morbidity, chorioamnionitis, gestational age at delivery, birthweight, or admission to neonatal intensive care. No information was provided on serious maternal morbidity or mortality.

There was some evidence that women managed in the hospital were more likely to have a caesarean section (RR 0.28, 95% CI 0.07 to 1.15). However, this result should be interpreted cautiously due to moderate heterogeneity (I² = 35%).

Women cared for at home spent about 10 fewer days in the hospital (mean difference -9.60, 95% CI -14.59 to -4.61) and were more satisfied with their care. Home care also had lower costs. However, these findings come from two small studies that lacked statistical power to detect meaningful differences between groups.

Larger randomized controlled trials are needed to measure differences in relevant outcomes, especially maternal satisfaction with care and cost analysis, which have social and economic implications for both developed and developing countries.
