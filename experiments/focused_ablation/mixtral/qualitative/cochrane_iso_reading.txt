ORIGINAL: We reviewed 11 studies with 414 participants. The studies compared different types of ultrasound therapy and other treatments. Many studies had a high or unclear risk of bias. Overall, there's not enough evidence to say one ultrasound regimen is better than another.

Two studies reported short-term overall improvement. A low-quality study with 68 participants found that, when compared to placebo, ultrasound may increase the chance of improvement after seven weeks of treatment. However, this result should be interpreted with caution due to high dropout rates and unadjusted data.

Another low-quality study with 60 participants found that, at three months post-treatment, ultrasound plus splint increased the chance of improvement compared to splint alone, but decreased the chance of improvement when compared to low-level laser therapy plus splint. However, participants were not blinded to treatment, and there were issues with randomization and potential unit of analysis error.

Differences in ultrasound frequencies, intensities, and combinations with other treatments showed little or no significant impact on symptoms, function, or neurophysiologic parameters. Only three studies reported adverse effects of ultrasound, so more data is needed to make conclusions about its safety.

There is poor quality evidence from limited data suggesting that ultrasound may be more effective than placebo for short- or long-term symptom improvement in people with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). There is insufficient evidence to support the use of one type of ultrasound regimen over another, or to show that ultrasound is more effective than other non-surgical treatments for CTS. More rigorous studies are needed to determine the effectiveness and safety of ultrasound for CTS.

There were no differences in serious neonatal morbidity, chorioamnionitis, gestational age at delivery, birthweight, or admission to neonatal intensive care. Information on serious maternal morbidity or mortality was not provided. Women in the hospital group were more likely to have a caesarean section (RR 0.28, 95% CI 0.07 to 1.15), but results should be interpreted cautiously due to moderate heterogeneity (I² = 35%). Women managed at home spent less time in the hospital (mean difference -9.60, 95% CI -14.59 to -4.61) and were more satisfied with their care. Home care also cost less. The two studies were small and underpowered to detect differences between groups. More large, powerful randomized controlled trials are needed to measure group differences for important pre-specified outcomes. These trials should focus on maternal satisfaction with care and cost analysis in both developed and developing countries.
